An office snack bot that can’t buy anything — and why that turned out to be the whole idea.

The ask

We want snacks. Let us make it easy and democratic.

Somebody has to buy them — and somebody has to decide which. That second part is where the office actually argues, and it’s the part no shared spreadsheet solves.

Wants always exceed the budget. A list tells you what people want; it doesn’t tell you what to buy.

What it actually is

Not a shopping list. An allocation system.

Budget cut line
Sorted by votes, filled to the cap. An item that doesn’t fit is skipped, not terminal — a cheap popular snack still gets in behind an expensive one.
Rollover queue
Everything below the line moves to next week with its votes. Popular things that just missed win the next round.
Past verdicts
A snack nobody ate carries a penalty forward, bounded at +1 / −2 so this week’s vote still outweighs history.

The bot itself. Nothing idles — it survives the laptop closing. ≈ Lambda + API Gateway
Cloudflare D1
SQLite at the edge, holding an append-only event log. No UPDATE, no DELETE, anywhere. ≈ Aurora Serverless
Cron Triggers
One hourly firing; the schedule lives in tested code, not in five cron expressions. ≈ EventBridge Scheduler
Ed25519 · WebCrypto
Every interaction is signature-verified and fails closed. An unverified endpoint is an open write path into the database.

Why it can’t just buy them

No consumer retailer lets a bot buy anything

  • Target ordering APIdoes not exist
  • Instacart public APIpartner-only, ~30–40 day key
  • Amazon Product Advertising APIclosed to new customers
  • Amazon multi-item cart URL302s into a sign-in gate
  • Amazon “Add to List”session + CSRF, not replayable
  • Headless browser checkoutbot detection, and a stored card

So a human clicks Place Order — and the bot never holds a payment credential.

No stored cards, no PCI exposure, nothing to breach. The approval step stopped being a limitation and became the design — which is why this survived the cart URL dying mid-build.

What reality did to the plan

Five bugs, all found by pasting real links

A $500 RAM kit
passed the non-food filter. Amazon files computer parts under pc — a slug no blocklist would guess. It now reads the breadcrumb department too.
A link without https://
became a 700-character SQL LIKE pattern. SQLite throws on those rather than returning nothing.
“That failed”
was the only diagnosis anyone got. Surfacing the real error is what found the bug above, one minute later.
The buyer bypass
disabled the filter for the one person most likely to paste links. It’s now a confirmation, not a bypass.

Unit tests covered every one of these paths and caught none of them. Each needed real Amazon data or a real paste.

What isn’t built

A full week
The Monday cron has never fired. Rollover and the escalating reminders are covered by tests, not by a real week.
Teams & Slack
Scoped, not started. Slack is 2–4 days; Teams 1–2 weeks. About 1,400 lines — the event log, the allocation, the scraping — don’t know what Discord is.
One-click ordering
Walmart’s cart URL returns blocked-as-a-bot rather than gone, so a real browser may work. That’s the one test a terminal can’t run.
DynamoDB
Still the long-term target. (week, sk) was chosen to mirror its partition-and-sort shape, so it’s one new file behind the same interface.

Thanks

The bot can’t buy anything. That’s why it’s safe to put in a channel.

It decides what to buy, which is the part people actually argue about — and a person always places the order.

Anything that touches a company card should go past InfoSec first. The human-in-the-loop design means no payment credentials live in the application, which is worth saying out loud rather than leaving them to ask.
